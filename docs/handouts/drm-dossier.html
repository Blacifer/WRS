Spring & Wagon QC — what it is, how it is run, and how we propose to start

A quality record for the spring section and the wagon POH line. It runs on one PC in the shop with no internet. Every figure on every screen is counted from the record; where the evidence is thin, the screen says so. A person decides — always. This document covers the roles, the security, the record and its logs, the photographs and their storage, what the system learns and how, what it needs, and a two‑week shadow run as the way in.

1What it is

Springs — the bench

The inspector checks the spring against the strip exactly as today and taps the band on the tablet: one tap per spring, the six bands of G‑95 Rev‑II with their heights printed on the buttons. A chime means serviceable, a buzz means condemned. The gauge in use is on every record, and a gauge with no calibration date marks its readings rather than stopping work. A crack, corrosion or deformation is condemned by its reason, and where photographing is on, the photograph is kept as the evidence. A wrong tap is undone with one tap, and the undo is recorded as a correction. Bogies with no published band table (LWLH25, LCCF20) take a typed height and are judged serviceable or condemned against WMM §309C — never against an invented table. Under the strip, Your record today: the inspector's own count, bands and the day's list.

Wagons — the pipeline

Each wagon is registered at entry and moves through seven stages to release. It carries its checklist by category (Springs, Wheels & Axles, Bearings, Brake System, Couplers & Draft Gear, Bogie Frame & Bolster, Friction Wedges, Body & Underframe), each item Pass / Fail / Condemned / Repaired / Replaced with the standard it cites; the wheel readings, judged against the drawing's limits as the chalk figure is typed; the bogie assembly photographs with a blind second pocket count; a parts ledger (what came off, went back on, was replaced or scrapped, by position); the air‑brake test proforma (WMM §720‑C, 14 rows, each judged as typed); and its stage history with dates and names.

Release

A wagon cannot be released with a condemned spring, a short pocket count, a wheel below the last‑shop‑issue diameter or a mandatory check undone — and the screen says which, with the figure and the limit. Advisories do not block, but each must be accepted by a named supervisor with a one‑time code, and the acceptance is on the certificate. The certificate is signed by the shop's own key and carries a QR that verifies on any phone with no server and no login.

The DRM

When today's pile finishes; how many bogies the sorted stock can build and which position limits it; where wagons wait and which one will miss its date; what keeps coming back; inspector quality; gauge exposure; the shop's springs against the standard. Every figure carries the count it rests on. Nothing on these screens is an estimate, a forecast or a model's number.

Inspector — does

The bench and the wagon: records, corrects, photographs, registers. Sees their own work and the wagon in front of them. Cannot release, cannot override, cannot see the dashboards.

Supervisor — decides

Everything the inspector does, plus the exit gate: release with a one‑time code, override with a reason and a code, the pocket‑count figures the inspector is not shown, stores, the shadow‑run log, the audit chain. For the pilot the supervisor's menu is kept to the shop floor.

DRM — oversees

Every dashboard, the standard report, the audit chain, the certificates, ask‑the‑records, the shadow run — read‑only. Cannot register or inspect a wagon, so a figure on the DRM's dashboard is never the DRM's own entry. The record is always written by the floor.

Administrator — maintains

Accounts and roles (switched off, never deleted, so attribution survives), the roster from a spreadsheet, checklist rules with reason and source, the gauge register with calibration papers, stores, the PC's readiness. Cannot release a wagon and cannot alter or delete any record.

4Security

Signing in and who did what

  • Every person has their own account and password (stored as a salted PBKDF2‑SHA‑512 hash; the shop's real passwords are set by the people themselves on first sign‑in; the demonstration passwords are refused by a production build unless the demonstration switch is deliberately set). A session lasts a working day and ends on sign‑out; the back button does not reopen a signed‑in screen.
  • Every write carries the signed‑in person's name, role, time and the tablet's address. There is no anonymous entry and no shared account.

The consequential actions need a second factor

  • Releasing a wagon, overriding a band or a stage, voiding a wagon registered in error, exporting the audit trail, creating an account: each asks for a one‑time code, valid five minutes, tied to that action and that person. The code comes from an authenticator app on the person's own phone once enrolled (no network needed), or, until then, is issued by the PC and must be typed back.
  • Every override records the new value, the original, the reason (ten characters at least) and the name. The record shows both.

The record cannot be altered

  • The audit log is append‑only by database trigger and hash‑chained: each entry carries the SHA‑256 of the previous entry, so an altered, inserted or removed entry breaks the chain. The audit screen re‑derives the whole chain on demand, in front of you, and says where it broke if it did. The parts ledger and the spring evidence are append‑only the same way. Corrections are recorded as corrections; nothing is overwritten.
  • The release certificate is signed with the shop's own Ed25519 key. Its QR is a link that verifies the signature in the reader's browser, with no server; the same page verifies a certificate file with no network at all. The wagon passport — the wagon's whole history, exported for the next shop — is a hash‑linked file the next shop can verify without contacting Raipur.

The network

  • Tablets reach the PC over https on the shop Wi‑Fi. The PC carries the workshop's own certificate authority; each tablet installs it once and trusts the PC for good, whatever address the PC gets. The server reissues its own certificate within thirty seconds of an address change.
  • Nothing leaves the shop. One PC, one database file, the photographs beside it. No cloud, no internet connection required at any point. One optional connection, switched on by the shop: Zapheit, our own service, which when a key is set receives manual questions and spoken sentences so a model can find the right clause or field. It never produces a figure or a verdict, and with the key removed the application does everything with keyword search and its own parser.

Backups

  • The PC takes a backup every night on its own: an AES‑256 encrypted copy of the database (PBKDF2, 210,000 iterations) with an HMAC to detect tampering, and the photographs encrypted the same way, to a second disk or USB drive kept elsewhere. The key file is kept apart from the backups.
  • A restore onto a second machine has been drilled and its result recorded; the readiness panel on the administrator's dashboard shows when the last backup was taken, where, and whether it sits on the same disk as the database (which it warns against).

6Photographs and storage

Where they go

  • On the shop PC, as files beside the database — photos\year\month\ — never on the tablet after sending, never outside the shop. Each row of the record keeps the file's SHA‑256 inside the audit chain, so a photograph altered or replaced on disk is reported as such rather than shown as evidence.
  • Carried with the nightly backup, encrypted the same way; restorable file by file, checked against the stored hash.

What they are for

  • Today: the evidence behind a condemned spring or a failed item; the four bogie assembly frames the pocket counts are made from; the wagon number read from the paint.
  • Later: the same photographs, labelled by the people who sorted them, are what the camera learns from — this shop's own springs, under this shop's light, with this shop's gauge post in frame (section 7).

Photographing every spring is a switch the inspector sets per session; the record does not depend on it. The shop can photograph condemned springs only, or everything — the space is there either way.

7What it learns, from what, and how far that goes

The honest sentence for the room: it learns from this shop's own photographs and corrections, it gets better as the shop uses it, and nothing changes on its own — every change is proposed with its evidence and accepted by a named supervisor, and the standard is never touched.

What is being built up from day one

  • Every spring sorted — band, height, gauge, inspector — some 150,000 a year at the shop's pace. This is what the standard report, the gauge‑drift check and the Stores forecast are computed from; each of those gets sharper as the count grows, and each says the count it rests on.
  • Every correction — a tap withdrawn, a reading the amber box questioned and the answer, a stencilled number the reader proposed and the person corrected, a spoken sentence read back and changed. The System Learning screen shows these per subsystem with accuracy and count; on the demonstration record every row reads "no data", because no one has used it yet. That is the truthful starting point.
  • Every photograph with its label — the band the inspector tapped while the camera watched, the bogie frame with its human count. These are the training material; they belong to the shop.

How the camera earns its place — in this order

  1. Evidence only. The camera photographs; the person taps the band. The photograph is filed against the tap. (Today.)
  2. Blind reads. The camera looks at a spring and says what it would call it, without being told the answer; the inspector's tap is the answer. Agreement is counted week by week — the share of the camera's own answers the inspector kept, allowed to fall. The DRM's dashboard shows this under "what a camera could tell an inspector".
  3. Proposing. Only after blind reads on this shop's own springs have shown the camera right often enough, over enough weeks, may it propose a band before the tap. The server refuses to accept a camera's band until that has been earned — this is enforced where the record is written, not on the tablet — and the screen says how far it is from earning it.
  4. Never deciding alone on a condemnation. A condemned spring always has a person's reason on it.

What is never learned

The G‑95 bands, the condemning limits, the wheel diameters, the brake‑test limits: these are regulation, transcribed and checked, not parameters. The learning screen says so in its footer. The only values the system may propose to tune are its own operational thresholds (how readily the amber box asks, how sure the number reader must be), and each proposal waits for a supervisor.

What "gets better with more photographs" means in numbers

The pocket count needs real bogie photographs from this shop before it can count reliably — the demonstration set is drawn. The spring camera needs blind reads on the order of several hundred before its agreement means anything, and the readiness of both is shown as a figure with its count, never as a percentage without one. A shop that photographs its springs through the shadow run will have the first honest reading of the camera by the end of it.

8Network, tablets, no internet

Nothing needs the internet

  • One PC in the spring section runs the application, the database, the photographs and the indexed manuals (ten documents, 8,200 passages, answered in their own words with the page cited).
  • Tablets on the shop Wi‑Fi — or one access point in the bay. Android tablets with a rear camera; the application installs to the home screen from the browser and opens like an app.
  • Each tablet installs the workshop certificate once. After that: no warning, and the camera, the microphone and offline use are allowed.

When the Wi‑Fi drops

  • The tablet keeps working. Readings are held on the tablet — the screen says how many are waiting — and send themselves when the network returns. Nothing is lost; nothing is recorded twice, even when a reply was lost mid‑way.
  • Mobile data does not reach the PC and is not needed; the tablets keep it off.
  • For the DRM at headquarters, a read‑only mirror of the nightly backup can run there; the live record never leaves the shop.

9What it needs, and how it is run day to day

Hardware

  • One Windows PC in the spring section — any ordinary PC of the last five years — on power and the shop Wi‑Fi. No server room.
  • Two Android tablets with rear cameras (₹15–20k each) to begin; more as the pilot widens.
  • A second disk or a USB drive for the encrypted backup, kept elsewhere.

Installing

From a USB stick: the Node runtime, the application folder, two double‑clicks. About ten minutes. The manuals are indexed once. No internet.

Running it

  • START keeps the server running and restarts it if it stops; it can be scheduled at boot so nobody touches it.
  • The backup takes itself every night; the readiness panel says whether it did.
  • Accounts, roles, the roster, checklist rules and the gauge register are the administrator's screens — no programmer is needed to add an inspector, a gauge or a shop line.
  • The manual is searched on the tablet; the answer quotes the page.

Support

Updates arrive as a new folder on a stick; the record and the photographs stay where they are. Nothing phones home.

10The way in — a two‑week shadow run

The proposal: two weeks in the spring section with the application running beside the register — both recording, nothing replaced. One PC, two tablets, two inspectors and one supervisor. At the end, the shadow‑run screen shows where the two disagreed and who was right. That decides go‑live.

Why two weeks and not a switch‑over

The application has been walked end to end on a built‑in record, but it has not yet met a real spring on this bench, this shop's light, this shop's inspectors at this shop's pace. Two weeks beside the register is enough for different inspectors, different shifts, different wagon types and several hundred springs to have gone through both paths — enough to trust a pattern, not a first clean day. The register stays the record of authority throughout.

What would stop go‑live

  • Any case where the application said serviceable and the register (or a re‑measurement) says condemned, or the reverse — a real classification disagreement, not rounding.
  • Any wagon the exit gate let through that the paper checklist would have caught, or blocked that paper would have passed.
  • Any gap — a spring or wagon on paper and not in the record, or against the wrong person.

What does not stop it, but is logged

A stencilled number the reader misread and the person corrected (expected — the manual entry is there for that); a screen somebody found confusing (feeds the quick‑start and the training, not the go/no‑go).

What we bring, and what we ask of the shop

  • The PC set up, the tablets trusted, the accounts made, the gauges entered, the manuals indexed — on day 0, by us.
  • A person reachable through the two weeks; fixes within a day for anything the floor finds.
  • The quick‑start, the role walks with pictures, and this document, printed.
  • A PC in the bay with power and Wi‑Fi (or the one we bring, on the shop's Wi‑Fi).
  • The names of two inspectors and one supervisor.
  • The shop's G‑95 Rev‑II and gauge register, to check against.
  • Twenty minutes of the reviewer's time at the end of each shift.

After go‑live — what keeps improving on its own record

The standard report fills in; the Stores forecast stops saying "not forecast yet" as condemnations accumulate; the gauge‑drift check has enough readings to speak; the camera's blind‑read agreement becomes a real figure; the wagon that will miss its date is computed from the shop's own stage times. Each of these is a screen that already exists and is waiting for the record.

11Not yet proven, said plainly

  • The camera on real springs. Every accuracy figure so far is from drawn test images. The shadow run is where the first real figure comes from, and the screen will show it with its count.
  • The pocket count on real bogie photographs. Same: the demonstration frames are drawn; the shop's frames are what it must learn from.
  • The bearing sound check has never heard a real defective CTRB. It is on the screen with that caveat and is not part of any release decision.
  • The shadow‑run verdict itself — which is what the two weeks are for.

The twenty‑minute demonstration, in order

  1. The bench (tablet, inspector): three springs by the band; undo one; the camera photographing; a spring condemned for a crack; the day's record.
  2. A wagon (tablet): WR/BCNHL/40112 → Photos: four frames, one needing a blind recount, the counter with no expected number.
  3. The gate (laptop, supervisor): the same wagon's short count as an advisory; SER/BOXNHL/30914 blocked by a condemned spring and the axle‑3 pair at 917.5 mm below 919; the wheels with the drawing beside them.
  4. Ask the Records (supervisor): which wagon type condemns the most snubbers; is any gauge reading high — and how it was computed.
  5. The DRM's screens: the shop floor right now; the standard report.
  6. The proof: the audit chain re‑derived; a certificate's QR verified on a phone with no server.
  7. The shadow run: a week of the log and the one case that decides.
